My Approach

Treatment begins through speaking.

Patients are invited to speak about what comes to mind, including what may feel uncertain, repetitive, difficult to articulate, or not yet fully understood. Through this process, one may begin to listen differently to what is being said, notice patterns that emerge over time, and encounter aspects of oneself with greater depth and clarity.

In treatment, reflection and introspection can deepen. Patterns may become clearer: how one relates to the self, how relationships are sustained, and where older ways of coping no longer serve. Change is not imposed; it is worked toward through attention, inquiry, and a closer engagement with one's thoughts, feelings, and recurring patterns.

Treatment is understood as an evolving process shaped through language, reflection, and the meanings carried in anxiety, sadness, anger, shame, longing, silence, conflict, and repetition. Psychoanalytic work invites a deeper engagement with oneself and offers the possibility of greater understanding over time.

About Nicole

A Reflective Approach to Treatment

Situated within a psychodynamic tradition and informed by psychoanalytic study and training, Nicole's clinical work as a Chicago psychoanalytic psychotherapist emphasizes careful attention to feeling, conflict, self-protection, relational patterns, and the meanings that emerge through treatment. She works with individuals experiencing anxiety, depression, and difficulties related to self-esteem and self-worth.

Her clinical attention turns toward recurring ways of relating, internal conflict, protective modes of coping, racial and cultural identity, and the ways experience may be carried, repeated, and expressed over time. Through speaking and reflection, aspects of experience that may otherwise go unrecognized can become available for deeper understanding.

Nicole holds a Master of Arts in Clinical Counseling and Psychotherapy from the Institute for Clinical Social Work. Her graduate studies have continued at the Boston Graduate School of Psychoanalysis, where she is completing a Master of Arts in Psychoanalysis while pursuing advanced psychoanalytic training.

She is a Licensed Clinical Professional Counselor (LCPC) in Illinois.

Areas of Focus

  • Anxiety

    Anxiety can appear as worry, tension, restlessness, or a persistent sense of danger that may not be easy to name. Treatment offers a setting to consider what the anxiety may be communicating and how it is connected to one's history, desires, conflicts, and relationships.

  • Depression

    Depression may involve sadness, emptiness, fatigue, withdrawal, or a loss of vitality and meaning. In therapy, these experiences can be explored with attention to grief, anger, disappointment, attachment, and the parts of life that may feel muted or unreachable.

  • Difficulties related to self-esteem and self-worth

    Struggles with self-worth often shape how a person receives care, criticism, success, intimacy, and disappointment. Psychoanalytic work can help illuminate the internal standards, early experiences, and relational patterns that influence how one comes to regard the self.

  • Racial and cultural identity

    Identity is lived through family, culture, race, language, belonging, exclusion, and social history. Treatment can provide a setting to speak freely about how these realities shape shame, pride, anger, grief, belonging, self-understanding, relationships, and the experience of being seen.

  • Relationship patterns and self-understanding

    Repeated relational difficulties can reveal important meanings about longing, protection, trust, loss, and vulnerability. Therapy can make these patterns more available for reflection so that new ways of understanding oneself and others may become possible.

About Treatment

Before beginning therapy.

What is psychoanalytic psychotherapy?

Psychoanalytic psychotherapy is a form of therapy that attends to anxiety, sadness, anger, shame, grief, desire, relationships, memory, repetition, and the meanings that emerge through speaking over time.

What happens during a first consultation?

A first consultation is an opportunity to speak about what brings you to therapy, ask initial questions, and begin considering whether the treatment feels like a thoughtful fit. It is a beginning conversation, not a commitment to ongoing work.

Do you offer therapy in Chicago?

Yes. Nicole Glass, MA, LCPC offers psychotherapy in Chicago, Illinois, with an office address in Hyde Park and appointment scheduling available online.

What issues do you work with?

Areas of focus include anxiety, depression, self-esteem and self-worth, racial and cultural identity, relationship patterns, and self-understanding.
